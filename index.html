A notebook, opened more than a century later

New Year’s Day, 1915 — and now

They packed for a picnic.

That’s the bit I can’t get past. Ore wagons with benches in them. Sandwiches. About a thousand people going to Silverton, and a girl my age on the train, sitting next to someone she liked.

They saw an ice-cream cart by the line, and a flag, and two men. Some of them thought the shots were for the new year. Then the shots weren’t.

Four people were killed. Seven were hurt. That night a crowd burned the German Club, and the police had to stand in front of a mosque so the same crowd wouldn’t go there next.

I keep looking for a clean reason. The notes they left don’t give me one I can live with. Neither does what the town did after.

The Battle of Broken Hill, 1 January 1915

Why did they do it, and was the backlash fair?

What is agreed

On the morning of 1 January 1915, a picnic train left Broken Hill for Silverton for the annual Manchester Unity Oddfellows picnic. The Barrier Miner, printed the same day, said the train left Sulphide-street station at 10 a.m., and that it consisted of two brake vans and 40 ore trucks, “with a freight of some 1200 picnickers on board.” Some later accounts say 41 carriages. This file records both, and does not choose.

Beside the line, an ice-cream cart was flying a small red flag with a white crescent and star — the Ottoman (Turkish) flag. Two men, later named as Gool Badsha Mahomed (the spelling of his name varies) and Mullah Abdullah, fired on the train. Four people were killed: Alma Priscilla Cowie, aged 17, a passenger; William John Shaw, aged 46, a passenger; Alfred Millard, who was riding a bicycle beside the line (his middle name is given as Elton in the NSW Police Gazette and some official reports, and as Elvin in some later accounts); and James Craig, aged 69, who was in his yard and was hit during the later gunfight, not on the train. Seven other people were wounded, including Constable Robert Mills.

Police, military men under Lieutenant Resch, and armed civilians pursued the two men to a quartz outcrop later known as White Rocks. Both men were shot. Abdullah was found dead. Gool was badly wounded and died in hospital. Sources disagree on how long the gunfight lasted: about 40 minutes in one later museum account; almost two hours in others.

Notes were found. Contemporary reports and later summaries do not all give the same wording. They agree that the notes linked the attack to the Ottoman Sultan and to the writers’ faith, and that Abdullah’s note also named a grudge against Chief Sanitary Inspector Cornelius Brosnan, who had prosecuted him for slaughtering animals away from the licensed abattoir. Gool’s note is widely reported to have been written for him by Abdullah, because Gool was illiterate. This file has not inspected the original notes.

That night a crowd in Broken Hill attacked and set fire to the German Club. Police later described the building as “maliciously set fire to and destroyed.” The club’s trustees offered a reward of £25; the state government matched it. Police and military were also sent to the North Camel Camp and the mosque, after a crowd moved in that direction. Officers later returned to the mosque because they had entered it without removing their boots.

Local Muslim residents were threatened. The two men’s bodies were buried by police at an undisclosed place. A coronial inquest on the four victims opened, was adjourned, and was held on 7 January 1915; an inquiry into the deaths of Gool and Abdullah followed the next day. The coroner found that the four had died from gunshot wounds feloniously inflicted by the two men, and that the shooting of the two men was justifiable.

Gool and Abdullah were Muslim men who had lived at the cameleers’ camp. Police records and newspapers did not agree on their nationality, and often called them Turks because of the flag. Later accounts describe Abdullah as probably from Afghanistan or a neighbouring region, literate in Dari, and Gool as an Afridi man from the Tirah region who had earlier served in the Ottoman army and, by 1915, sold ice-cream in Broken Hill. The Ottoman Empire had entered the war against Britain in late October 1914.

Three witnesses

The same three questions for each: What did they say they saw? · Could they really have seen it? · Why might they say it? A period portrait is used where one exists and is free to reuse. Otherwise you will see a plain silhouette, not a likeness. Tap a picture to enlarge it.

M. Kenny

Passenger on the picnic train · Electrician on the water-supply works · Named in The Barrier Miner, 1 January 1915

A named passenger, close enough to see the cart, the flag, and the rifles, whose account was printed the day it happened.

Source. The Barrier Miner (Broken Hill), 1 January 1915, “War in Broken Hill,” as reprinted from the same-day report. This card follows that report’s wording, and does not add details from later retellings. No period portrait of M. Kenny has been located for this file. The image is a silhouette, not a likeness.

What they said. The Barrier Miner reported that the two men “attracted the attention of Mr. M. Kenny, who was a passenger on the train.” Kenny, “engaged on the water supply works in the capacity of electrician, thought at first that there most be something wrong with the main, and that these men were attending to the damage. He then saw that they had rifles in their hands, pointed at the train, and almost simultaneously he saw and heard the rifles fired.” The paper said the firing continued while the train passed, “20 or 30 shots being fired in all,” and that the men “could be plainly seen to be either Turks or Afghans.”

Could they really have seen it? He was on the train, close to the water main and the cart. He could see what was in front of him that morning. He could not see the notes in the men’s belts, or what happened at the German Club that night, or why the two men had come. A newspaper report, even a same-day one, is still a reporter’s wording of what Kenny said.

Why might they say it? He was asked, or he offered, an eyewitness account of a public shock. A man who first thought of a broken water main is useful, because it shows how ordinary the scene looked until it wasn’t. It is still one pair of eyes, from one wagon, printed in a town already reaching for the word “Turks.”

The notes left by Gool Badsha Mahomed and Mullah Abdullah

Written statements found after the shooting · 1 January 1915 · Wording differs between reports

The two men’s own last words, as newspapers and later writers reported them. They are evidence. They are also translations, and they do not all match.

Source. Contemporary newspaper reports and later summaries of the notes, including accounts that Gool’s note was written for him by Abdullah. Wording is not identical across sources. This card uses short, widely reported fragments, and does not stitch them into a single invented letter. This file has not inspected the original notes held in the archives. No death photographs are used. The image is a silhouette, not a likeness of either man.

What they said. Reports agree that the notes tied the attack to the Ottoman Sultan and to the writers’ faith. One widely quoted fragment from Gool’s note is: “I must kill you and give my life for my faith.” Some later accounts give a longer wording, including a claim to hold the Sultan’s order. Abdullah’s note, as later summarised, said he was dying for his faith and in obedience to the Sultan, “but owing to my grudge against Chief Sanitary Inspector Brosnan, it was my intention to kill him first.” Brosnan had prosecuted him for slaughtering animals away from the licensed abattoir, which Abdullah used so that the meat would be halal. William John Shaw, who died on the train, also worked in the sanitary department; he is not the man named in the note.

Could they really have seen it? They could write, or have written, what they wanted people to believe about their motives. They could not be cross-examined. A translation can shift a sentence. A note that mentions both a holy war and a local grudge does not tell you which reason, if either, came first. Later claims that a Sultan’s letter was forged, or that the flag was planted, are disputed, and are flagged in Super Sleuths.

Why might they say it? To leave a reason behind, and to place the killing inside a war and a faith — and, in Abdullah’s case, inside a quarrel with an inspector. A last note can be a confession, a claim, or a justification. It cannot, by itself, tell you whether the town’s answer that night was fair.

Inspector Miller, and the police record of that night

Broken Hill police · The German Club, the camel camp, and the inquest · January 1915

The official response after the shooting: a pursuit, a fire, a guard on a mosque, and a coroner’s finding.

Source. The Barrier Miner, 1 January 1915 and following days, as reported and later summarised, including the police description of the German Club as “maliciously set fire to and destroyed,” the reward offered by the club’s trustees and matched by the state, the guard on the North Camel Camp and mosque, and the later conciliatory visit after officers had entered the mosque in boots. The coronial findings of 7–8 January 1915 are reported in later official and newspaper accounts, including the NSW Police Gazette, 17 February 1915. No period portrait of Inspector Miller is used here. The image is a silhouette, not a likeness.

What they said. The Barrier Miner said Inspector Miller sent police to the scene as soon as the alarm was telephoned in. That night, after a crowd set fire to the German Club, police and military were sent to the mosque at the North Camel Camp “to preserve order,” because they “feared a repetition of the proceedings” at the club. A crowd of some hundreds was held off. Days later, Inspector Miller and Captain Hardie visited the mosque again, after officers had entered it without removing their boots. The coroner found the four deaths felonious, and the shooting of Gool and Abdullah justifiable. The government matched a reward for information about the burning of the club, as a statement against vigilantism.

Could they really have seen it? Miller could describe what his men were sent to do, and what they found. He was not on the picnic train when the first shots were fired, and he could not see inside every part of a night crowd. A police report, and a coroner’s finding, are official. They are also frames: they can tell you that the club was burned, and still leave open how fair the town’s anger was.

Why might they say it? To keep order, to show that the two men were stopped, and to show — after the fire — that a riot would not be treated as nothing. Officials had reasons to calm a town and to defend their own conduct. Students can set the guard on the mosque beside the fact that a crowd had already burned a building, and beside the later protest when police entered the mosque in boots.

Why it still matters

The “Battle of Broken Hill” is still told as a curiosity of the First World War on Australian soil, and sometimes as if it were simple. The file is open because the notes do not all say the same thing, because a picnic train is not a battlefield, and because the night that followed — a burned club, a guarded mosque, a secret burial — is part of the same case. How a town answers a crime, and who it answers it upon, still matters.

2. Names, numbers, and how long it lasted

Alma, not Elma: some later stories use Elma; the inquest and the Police Gazette use Alma Priscilla Cowie. Alfred Millard’s middle name is Elton in some official reports and Elvin in some later accounts. The Barrier Miner said 40 ore trucks and “some 1200” picnickers; some later accounts say 41 carriages. Museum and later accounts disagree on whether the gunfight lasted about 40 minutes or almost two hours.

Question. Which of these disagreements changes the case question, and which is only a reminder to check a spelling?

3. The notes do not all match

This file has not inspected the original notes. Later writers give longer or shorter wordings. Some accounts say three notes were found, and that not all survive in the archive. A later claim, reported as coming from “Turkish sources,” that a Sultan’s letter was forged and the flag planted, is not treated as agreed. It is a claim, and it needs a named source before it can go further.

Question. If two translations of a last note disagree, what can you still say for sure, and what should you leave open?

4. James Craig was not on the train

Three people were killed in the attack on the train and the pipe track: Alma Cowie, William Shaw, and Alfred Millard. James Craig was in his yard, about a hundred yards from the Cable Hotel, and was hit during the later gunfight. A stepdaughter urged him to come inside. Treating all four deaths as if they happened in the ore wagons flattens the day.

Question. Why does it matter, for a finding, where each of the four was when they were hit?

5. Walhanna Asson, and the camel camp

Walhanna Asson, a Punjabi-speaking camel-owner from Peshawar, carried water to a wounded constable while under fire, and was himself nearly shot by late-comers who took him for “one of the enemy” until police protected him. That night a crowd moved on the mosque. Officers later went back because they had entered it in boots. Local Muslims protested when a gravedigger began two graves in their cemetery for the two attackers. The backlash did not fall only on a club that happened to be German.

Question. If you are asked whether the backlash was fair, whose evidence belongs in the answer — the burned club, the guarded mosque, Asson, or all of them?

6. Were “all Germans” already interned?

Some later retellings say the German Club stood empty because every German in Australia had already been interned. That is too strong for 1 January 1915. Internment of “enemy aliens” happened during the war, and it was not a single clean sweep on one day. The club was burned. German residents were threatened. How many people were in the club that night, and how internment later widened, should be checked, not assumed.

Question. What happens to a story about backlash if you write, wrongly, that the target building was already empty of the people it stood for?

7. Sure, maybe, or a guess?

Sure: a picnic train was fired on on 1 January 1915; four people were killed; Gool and Abdullah were shot dead; the German Club was burned that night; a mosque was guarded. Maybe: the exact wording of every sentence in the notes; how long the gunfight lasted; how far a later “planted flag” claim can go. A guess: a single clean motive, or the private thoughts of Alma Cowie. The opener is not evidence of 1915 feeling, except as a later reader’s.

Teacher notes

What’s true about the opener

The typographic page is a constructed Year 10 student notebook, written for this file, in an angsty voice. It is not a 1915 diary and not the voice of a named historical person. “A girl my age” is Alma Priscilla Cowie, 17; the opener does not invent her thoughts or last words. Violence is named, not described in graphic detail. No death photographs are used anywhere in the file.

Curriculum

The brief asked for an accurate AC9HS9 code. There is no AC9HS9. Year 9 History uses AC9HH9 codes. The lead code is AC9HH9K11: the effects of the First World War on Australian society. The shooting happened on Australian soil in January 1915, after the Ottoman Empire had entered the war; the aftermath includes a riot, a burned German Club, and the treatment of a local Muslim community. AC9HH9K09 (places Australians fought) is not used as the lead, because this was not a campaign overseas.

Source conflicts, flagged

Alma / Elma Cowie; Alfred Elton / Elvin Millard; 40 trucks / 41 carriages; “some 1200” picnickers; gunfight of about 40 minutes / almost two hours; varied spellings of Gool Badsha Mahomed (Gool, Goul, Mahomed, Mohammed, Mahommed). Translations of the notes do not all match. Later “planted flag” / forged letter claims are not treated as agreed. Overstatements that “every German” had already been interned are flagged. Shaw worked in the sanitary department; the man named in Abdullah’s note is Inspector Brosnan, not Shaw.

Religion, race, and care

Gool and Abdullah were Muslim. The local cameleer community was not the same as the two men. Teach the faith, and the camp, with respect. Do not let the word “Turk,” which 1915 papers used because of the flag, erase that uncertainty. Living families, including descendants of victims and of the camel camp, deserve legal and ethical care even though the principals are long dead. Year 9–10 students can hold two things at once: the killing of civilians was a crime, and a riot against other people’s club, camp and mosque still has to be judged.

Country

Broken Hill is on Wilyakali Country. This file does not tell a First Nations history of the event, and does not invent one. A deeper local treatment would need consultation. The student pages do not pretend otherwise.

Pictures

No portraits of Kenny, Miller, Gool or Abdullah are used. Silhouettes are labelled as silhouettes, not likenesses. Police or morgue photographs, if they exist, are not used. The QR train is an emblem, not a picture of the 1915 picnic train.
